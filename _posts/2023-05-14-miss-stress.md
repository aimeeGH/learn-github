The following essay follows Mia, a third year finance major who is taking a class on the mind-body connection. Here, she examines the impact childhood piano lessons had on her, embedding quotes from her class's research.
The public pianos at UCLA are always occupied, whether it's by the novice pianist who can barely read sheet music or the music major who can casually play Chopin's Fantaisie-Impromptu. On this particular Friday, however, the piano sat without its pianist and began calling my name, like a siren in the distance. Cautiously, I approached, but as I got closer, the soft whisper crescendoed into a jarring scream. Instantly, I was overcome with a wave of stress and nostalgia. My fight-or-flight response kicked in; should I sit down and play or run in the other direction?
I don't often tell people this, but I was a classically trained pianist. I say was because I have since fallen out of practice and feel mixed emotions about my time as one. When my teacher, 老师, first heard me play at the age of five, she told my mom, "She has a gift." Although looking back, I wonder if it was a way to convince my mom to pay for lessons. Either way, it worked, and my mom made it abundantly clear that piano lessons were expensive, and she was taking time off to drive me. If she heard from 老师 that I hadn't played well, she would scold me all the way home.
The thing is, I loved expressing myself through music and performing at recitals, but dreaded my strict teacher and the feeling of never being good enough. Every Monday after school, my mom would drop me off in front of my teacher's Victorian-style home, where she had every light turned off, except for a single lightbulb that hung above the grand piano. A glass of water placed on the lid of the piano would vibrate as I played. With my mind racing and palms sweating, I prayed that my hours of practice would get even a subtle nod of approval from 老师. For the next forty-five minutes, all my brain's energy was devoted to my repertoire. "My body disappeared into the background as I became [acutely aware] of the multitude of fine motor adjustments [in my hands]," as if the only things that mattered were my ten fingers and the eighty-eight keys (Johnson 5). Even with my body's full effort, it was never enough, and I remember the tears streaming down my cheeks in the backseat of my mom's car, only for the whole process to repeat the next week.
Piano was a source of stress for me growing up, and I counted down the days when I would never be forced to face the instrument again. When I finally moved away for college, I treated it like a "toxin to be eliminated from [my psyche]," dismissing any attempts to play (McGonigal 38).
However, as much as I suppress my musical past, I often find myself listening to classical piano to unwind from a stressful day. When I hit play on my carefully curated classical playlist, I can already feel my posture adjust, breathing settle, and joints unstiffen. My scattered thoughts fall in line, as if for the first time that day, I had the motivation to fold my laundry and the clarity to start writing this paper. Some days, I can almost feel 老师 hovering over me, keeping me focused on the task at hand.
As much as piano lessons caused me distress, playing and listening to classical music acts as eustress that "improved mental function, boosted memory, and enhanced health" (Rudland 41). At first, it felt paradoxical that combating school stress with childhood distress created all these body-mind benefits. In a way, it's my brain micro-dosing my body to past stressful experiences, "giving me the energy [and urgency] to rise to [life's many] challenges" (McGonigal 50). If I could survive a decade's worth of piano lessons, I could get through whatever life throws my way.
Environment plays an integral role in how I engage with stressors, distress, and eustress. For instance, when 老师 organized group recitals, her quiet, dark house transformed into a boisterous, vibrant theatre. Suddenly, the lightbulb above the piano became a spotlight. Instead of sweating with worry, my hands were anxious to deliver an unforgettable performance. Instead of hunching over the hard piano bench, 老师 allowed her students to bounce on exercise balls to the beat of the music. "Replacing the typical affordances of sitting, with new ones [bouncing], inspired [musical] creativity" (Rietveld 932). The group recital environment also afforded me a break from the strict technical rules of private lessons in exchange for the musical expression that first made me fall in love with the piano.
Instead of stressing over 老师's harsh criticism, my mind "brought [my] attention into the present moment, [focusing] on" the sequined dress my mom got from the Macy's clearance section, the harmonized cheering of the other students, and the beautiful Yamaha grand piano waiting for me to play (Winston 5:55). This mindfulness soothed my nerves of performing in front of a crowd. When it was finally my turn to play, my body was ready, and my mind was present, so I consequently performed much better.
Unlike the keys of a piano, stress isn't just black and white. In fact, according to psychologist Dr. Kelly McGonigal, there are many aspects of the stress response. While most know of the fight-or-flight response, McGonigal clarifies, "It does not fully encapsulate how humans deal with stress in the [modern world]" (47). After reading about The Upside of Stress, I pushed myself to tap into all aspects of my stress response. I put on my headphones and hit play on some Mozart, allowing my fingers to unclench and dance to the flow of the piece.
First, I envision 老师 telling me my technique was not up to her standards. Immediately, I feel a shot of cortisol pump through my veins. Perhaps it is my innate need for validation or my people-pleasing nature, but regardless, my challenge response is activated. I am dialed in, ready to show my imaginary piano teacher what I am capable of. The nerves and anxiety that once consumed me now give me the resilience I need to take on the source of my stress. Next, I lock in my excite and delight response, thinking about how accomplished I'd feel after overcoming this major stressor and looking forward to the hike I was going on this weekend. I also voice the source of my anxieties over a call with my sister, accessing my tend and befriend response, seeking out those social bonds I often put on the back burner to academics and hustle culture. Finally, I show self-compassion toward my mind and body, setting social media time limits on my phone to remind myself "[physical and mental] comparison between oneself and others is not needed to enhance or defend self-esteem" (Neff 87). By engaging all aspects of the stress response, I use it as a grounding technique to bring my boiling anxiety to a simmer.
As much as I try to suppress my musical past, my body has piano withdrawals every so often. My fingers crave the reliable keys, my foot yearns for the soft pedal, and my ears long for the demanding metronome. The worst of the withdrawals - nostalgia. I associate piano lessons with simpler times; back when I didn't have the stress of finding a job, doing my taxes, and learning how to adult. The further away I move from home, the more compassion I feel for Mom and 老师. As in many immigrant households, tough love is considered a love language. Deep down, I know they believed in me and my potential, but they, too, were inflicted by "the wounds of [a larger] cultural shadow" (Remen 35). They are immigrants, projecting their lost hopes and aspirations onto the next generation.
She taught me much more than how to play the piano, but how to be a diamond under pressure.
While I hear the public pianos around campus calling my name, I realize they are not pressuring me to sit down and play, but rather reminding me that they are here whenever I'm ready.
